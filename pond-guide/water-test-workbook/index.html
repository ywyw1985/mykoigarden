Read a koi pond water test

A pH of 8.2, hard water, a result marked ND. What does each reading actually tell you? Try three fictional pond records. Open a possible interpretation to see how it fits the measurements.

Practice record 1 / 3

The sunny afternoon sample

The same person tested the same spot at two times on consecutive days with the same meter. The meter was checked against its calibration buffers.

TimeDay 1Day 2
06:307.37.4
16:308.18.2

Which reading of the record fits?

The afternoon pH rose by 0.8 on both days.

Supported by the measurements Each morning-to-afternoon pair rose by 0.8 pH units. Pond plants and algae can remove carbon dioxide during daylight and drive pH up. These two days suggest a repeatable daily pattern.

The jump from 7.3 to 8.2 shows a 0.9 rise from one morning to the next.

Look again Those are different times of day. The two morning measurements were 7.3 and 7.4, only 0.1 apart.

How to check

Sample the same spot and depth near dawn and again late in the day. Compare dawn with dawn to assess a multi-day trend. Record recent weather and keep the two times separate.

Practice record 2 / 3

One sample, two kinds of hardness

A keeper uses two different titration kits on the same pond sample. Both kit cards give results in mg/L as calcium carbonate.

Test labelPond reading
KH / alkalinity test36 mg/L as CaCO₃
GH / general hardness test143 mg/L as CaCO₃

Which reading of the record fits?

The higher GH guarantees the pond will resist a pH change.

Look again GH mainly reflects dissolved calcium and magnesium. That reading alone tells little about how much acid the pond can neutralize.

The KH test gives more relevant information about buffering.

Supported by the measurements The tests use a common reference unit but measure different properties. The alkalinity test measures acid-neutralizing capacity. The general hardness test mainly reflects calcium and magnesium.

How to check

Read the full name on each bottle. Use a fresh sample for each kit and follow its volume and endpoint instructions. Copy the unit shown on its chart. A value expressed in degrees needs conversion before comparing with mg/L.

Practice record 3 / 3

Same total ammonia, different afternoon

A freshwater sample is tested before and after the pond warms. TAN means total ammonia nitrogen, including the nitrogen in both NH₃ and NH₄⁺. The separate free-ammonia method reports ND below its stated detection limit of 0.02 mg/L as NH₃.

SamplepH / water temperatureResult on report
Morning · TAN7.2 / 18 °C0.20 mg/L as N
Afternoon · TAN8.2 / 24 °C0.20 mg/L as N
Same afternoon · free NH₃8.2 / 24 °CND

Which reading of the record fits?

ND shows the afternoon water contained exactly zero NH₃.

Look again ND means the free-ammonia method did not detect NH₃ at its stated limit. A smaller amount could be present. Keep the ND qualifier and the limit in the record.

The afternoon sample can have a larger free-ammonia fraction even though the TAN results match.

Supported by the measurements At the higher pH and temperature, a larger share of the same TAN is expected as un-ionized NH₃. The 0.20 result is total nitrogen in the two ammonia forms. It is not a measured 0.20 mg/L of free NH₃.

How to check

Retest a fresh sample, following the kit’s timing and lighting instructions. Read the chemical name, reporting basis and detection limit. Measure pH and temperature at the same time. If a color matches a “0” square, record the chart reading and its resolution.

Reading the units

In dilute fresh water, 1 ppm is approximately 1 mg/L for the same substance on the same reporting basis. pH has no unit. Keep qualifiers like “as N” and “as CaCO₃” with the value when copying or comparing results.
