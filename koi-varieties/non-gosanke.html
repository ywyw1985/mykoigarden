Variety Guide

Koi varieties beyond Gosanke

Gosanke means Kohaku, Taisho Sanke and Showa Sanshoku. For other koi, start with the base color, then look at the markings, overall luster and scale coverage. These clues help you choose the right guide.

Major groups to learn next

How to read show classes

Ginrin describes reflective scales, Doitsu describes reduced scale coverage, and longfin describes fin shape. These traits can occur across color patterns. Show classes and variety names follow the organizer’s rules. The 62nd ZNA show, for example, lists Muji separately and groups Ginrin Tancho with Tancho.

Kawarimono family map

Zen Nippon Airinkai organizes Kawarimono into practical families. This helps avoid treating every unusual koi as an unrelated “miscellaneous” fish.

FamilyNamed varietiesWhat the grouping tells you
Karasugoi familyKarasugoi, Hajiro, Hageshiro, Yotsushiro, Matsukawabake, Kumonryu, SuminagashiBlack-based relatives that must be separated by white placement, scale type, and pattern character.
Matsuba familyAka Matsuba, Ki Matsuba, Shiro Matsuba, Doitsu MatsubaThe dark scale centers form a pinecone-like effect over the ground color.
Kage Utsuri familyKage Shiro Utsuri, Kage Hi Utsuri, Kage Ki Utsuri, Kage ShowaA thin, mesh-like sumi pattern lies over the white, red, or yellow markings. This is different from relying only on the heavier Utsuri-zumi pattern.
KanokoKanoko Kohaku, Kanoko Sanke, Kanoko Showa, Kanoko Asagi, Kanoko Kin ShowaRed is localized to individual scales, producing a dotted pattern while the underlying variety remains recognizable. Kanoko Kin Showa also has whole-body metallic luster.
Single-colorKigoi, Aka Muji, Aka Hajiro, Karashigoi, Chagoi, SoragoiBody shape, skin quality, and even color matter more than a complex pattern.
Ochiba familyOchiba Shigure, KoyoAutumnal brown or yellow patterning distinguishes these fish from plain single-color koi.
Recent named combinationsGinrin Koromo Showa; Ginrin Kanoko Karashi (“Golden Corn”)These names combine an underlying color-pattern identity with a reflective Ginrin scale trait; they should not be read as simple single-color varieties.

Kanoko, Kage, Gin Rin, or ordinary color?

These labels describe different visual structures. Check where the color or reflection sits before naming the fish.

FeatureWhat to look forUseful distinction
KanokoRed color is concentrated on individual scales, creating separated dots.Unlike an ordinary hi plate, the red is not one continuous field. Kanoko Kohaku may also carry ordinary continuous red markings.
KageThin, mesh-like sumi appears within the underlying white, red, or yellow markings.Read the reticulated “shadow” together with the base Showa or Utsuri pattern, including its stronger Utsuri-zumi.
Gin RinIndividual scales reflect gold or silver light.The effect belongs to the scales and can vary with viewing angle and lighting.
Metallic / HikariLuster carries across the body rather than appearing only as isolated reflective scales.Kanoko Kin Showa is both Kanoko-patterned and metallic; one label does not cancel the other.

Other verified Kawarigoi names

Goshiki Shigure
Shows Asagi-style reticulation, yellow markings, and gentle overall luster.
Cha Utsuri
A black-bodied Utsuri type with brown or reddish-brown markings and recognizable Utsuri-zumi.
Hajiro
Jet-black body with white pectoral fins; some individuals retain a little black within the pectoral fins.
Beni Matsukawabake
Combines red pattern with Matsukawabake characteristics.

Kumonryu and Matsukawabake caution: their black pattern can change markedly with the environment, sometimes over a short period. A photograph records the fish at that moment, not a guaranteed lifelong pattern.

Golden Corn and Ginrin Koromo Showa

The All Japan Nishikigoi Promotion Association recognizes Ginrin-kanoko-karashi, commonly called Golden Corn. Its mustard-colored body carries reflective golden or silver scales and a reddish, dotted Kanoko pattern on selected scales. Some examples also show mustard-colored markings together with the red dotted pattern.

Ginrin-koromo-showa combines golden or silver reflective scales with Koromo-showa characteristics. In both names, Ginrin identifies the reflective scale effect; it does not replace the underlying pattern identity.

Do not identify Golden Corn from yellow color alone. The Ginrin reflection and the red Kanoko-style dots are part of the official description, and a whole-fish view is preferable to a close crop.
