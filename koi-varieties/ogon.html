Variety Guide

Ogon koi

Ogon are metallic koi best known for their even color and sheen across the whole body. Yamabuki Ogon are yellow-gold and Platinum Ogon are metallic white to light gray. Both belong to Hikari-muji, a group that also includes Kin and Gin Matsuba.

Yamabuki Ogon photograph

How to recognize Ogon koi

Ogon are appreciated for metallic luster and clean simplicity. Because there is no complex pattern to distract the eye, body shape, even color, and shine are especially important.

FeatureWhat to look forCommon issue
Metallic lusterEven shine from head through tail, including fins.Dull head or uneven sheen that breaks the metallic impression.
ColorClean, consistent single color: platinum, yamabuki yellow, orange, or related Ogon tones.Patchy color or stains that look accidental rather than variety-related.
BodyStrong body is critical because there is no pattern to hide weakness.Buying a bright color on a thin or bent frame.
Viewing angleCheck the sheen as the fish swims and turns.Surface reflections can make a still photo harder to interpret.

Core terms

Hikari
Metallic shine or luster. On Ogon, weak shine is easy to see because the body is one color.
Hikari-muji
The metallic group with no large body markings. It includes Yamabuki Ogon and the scale-level pattern of Kin and Gin Matsuba.
Even color
Patchy color or dull areas weaken the impression because there is no pattern to hide them.

Where Hikari-muji fits

Metallic koi are easier to separate when you look beyond shine. JNPA uses the body ground, large color markings, and utsuri-zumi to distinguish three major Hikari groups.

GroupIdentification ruleExamples
Hikari-mujiNo large color imprints; the whole body has metallic luster. Scale-level Matsuba centers can still be present.Yamabuki Ogon, Platinum Ogon, Kin Matsuba
Hikari-moyoNon-black ground, visible color markings, and whole-body metallic luster.Hariwake, Sakura Ogon, Kikusui
Hikari-utsuriBlack ground, visible markings, whole-body metallic luster, and Utsuri-style sumi.Kin Showa, Gin Shiro Utsuri, Kin Ki Utsuri

Matsuba is the important exception: the dark center of each scale forms a repeated pinecone texture, not a large body-color imprint. That scale-level texture does not automatically move Kin Matsuba or Gin Matsuba into Hikari-moyo. ZNA likewise treats Matsuba markings as scale texture rather than an ordinary pattern in its Muji show grouping.

Four net-like patterns that are not the same

Several koi can look “netted” in photographs, but the location and material of the dark detail are different. Check where the dark color sits before naming the variety.

AppearanceWhere the dark detail sitsDecisive extra clue
MatsubaDark pigment is centered within individual scales, repeating as a pinecone texture.Aka and Ki Matsuba are non-metallic Muji; Kin and Gin Matsuba add whole-body metallic luster.
AsagiA blue ground and orderly scale mesh extend across the body.Look for a clean head and balanced red on the belly, fins, and cheeks.
KujakuAn Asagi-like mesh appears beneath red markings.The whole body must also show metallic luster.
KagePale, mesh-like sumi appears inside an existing red, white, or yellow pattern field.It is an overlay within the pattern, not a dark center on every scale.

This distinction helps prevent strong lighting from turning ordinary scale reflection into “metallic,” or any repeated dark texture into Matsuba.

Named Hikari-muji varieties

Hikari-muji includes more than the familiar yellow-gold Ogon. These names describe the base color, scale treatment, or distinctive head marking.

VarietyIdentification point
Yamabuki OgonBright yellow-gold metallic body without large color markings.
Platinum OgonWhite to light-gray metallic body without large color markings.
Nezu OgonUniform silver-gray metallic body.
Orange OgonReddish-yellow or orange metallic body without large color markings.
Mukashi OgonTurmeric to dark-gold metallic ground, with black appearing on parts of the scales, fins, and head.
Kin MatsubaGold or turmeric metallic ground with a dark pinecone-like matsuba pattern across the back.
Gin MatsubaSilvery-white metallic ground with a matsuba pattern across the back.
Doitsu Gin MatsubaThe reduced-scale Doitsu form of Gin Matsuba, with exposed skin and the same silver Matsuba character.
Aka Matsuba OgonRed or vermilion metallic ground with a matsuba pattern across the back.
Mizuho OgonA Doitsu red-orange metallic form with exposed skin and some black-shining scales; also called Doitsu Aka Matsuba Ogon.
Kin KabutoGold-brown metallic body with paired gold head markings that resemble the crest of a Japanese helmet.
Gin KabutoSilver metallic body with paired silver helmet-crest markings on the head.

Beginner notes

  • Look for strong, even metallic sheen from head through tail.
  • Color should look consistent, not patchy or dull.
  • A good Ogon can be very visible and satisfying in a garden pond.
  • Because Ogon are visually simple, do not compromise on body line and swimming behavior.

Reference

Classification and identification points follow the All Japan Nishikigoi Promotion Association's Hikari-muji guide, its references for Asagi, Kujaku, and Kage varieties, plus the Zen Nippon Airinkai Muji classification note. See also JAS 0020 terminology (PDF).
