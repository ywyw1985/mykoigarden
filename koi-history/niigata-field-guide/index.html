Where koi got their colors: Ojiya and Yamakoshi

In central Ojiya you can follow a single koi as it turns. In the hills of Yamakoshi, the fish disappear from view and the ponds come into focus. These three stops tell the same story at two different scales.

How to visit

The two Ojiya stops are in the Jōnai neighborhood. Arrange transport for the mountain stop in Yamakoshi, Nagaoka. Use the official pages below for access and seasonal notices. Observe from public sites; arrange a visit with a breeder before entering a working farm.

  1. Nishikigoi no Sato
  2. The pond beside Ojiya City Hall
  3. Nikoniko Hiroba

Japan Tourism Agency on Yamakoshi carp farms

A region and a date

The historical district called Nijūmuragō included eastern Ojiya and part of present-day Nagaoka. Niigata Prefecture places the selection of colored offspring from food carp in this region around the early nineteenth century. There is no precise birthday or single first pond in that account. The 1914 Tokyo Taishō Exhibition is a separate, dated event: it introduced these already-established fish to a much larger audience.

Niigata Prefecture’s history of nishikigoi

  1. Nishikigoi no Sato

    1-8-22 Jōnai, Ojiya

    Begin in the exhibition room with the displays on breeding and varieties, then go to the viewing pool. Pick one fish with a distinctive patch behind the head and follow it through a turn. Which mark helped you recognize it when its body changed direction? The museum brings fish into clear water, where you can answer that question at close range.

    Museum rooms and viewing ponds

  2. The pond beside Ojiya City Hall

    2-7-5 Jōnai, Ojiya

    Look beside the main entrance steps for a pond and an illustrated sign. The city describes Kohaku, Taisho Sanshoku and Showa Sanshoku here. Compare the pictures on the sign before you try to match a swimming fish to one of them. The pond opened in 2016, two years after Ojiya chose koi as its city fish to mark recovery from the 2004 earthquake.

    Ojiya City’s pond and identification board

  3. Nikoniko Hiroba

    759 Mushigame, Yamakoshi, Nagaoka

    From this public overlook, follow the banks between the hillside plots. Two local words explain what you see: tanada are terraced rice fields and tanaike are terraced ponds. Both can reflect the sky. From a distance, an expanse of open water alone is poor evidence that fish are present. This stop is about learning to read a working landscape.

    Nagaoka Tourism’s viewpoint and access information

How snow became a summer water supply

A snowy winter did not guarantee a steady supply of water on the hillsides in summer. Families stored snowmelt and dug horizontal wells to tap underground water. Rice fields and carp ponds shared the benefits of that local work. Japan recognized this rice-and-carp system as national agricultural heritage in 2017.

Japan’s agriculture ministry on the Chūetsu system

Why see the fish in town before climbing into the hills?

Clear viewing water lets you study one fish’s markings. The hillside shows the banks and stored water that made generations of fish keeping possible. Pairing the two observations explains much more than trying to identify small fish from a distant viewpoint.
