Carp in Japanese art: two ways to paint movement

One carp holds its head against a waterfall. Two others hover among pond plants. Look closely at two Japanese hanging scrolls in The Metropolitan Museum of Art and see how a painter can make still silk feel like moving water.

Carp and WaterweedsA Carp Ascending a Waterfall

Yōgetsu · Japan, late 15th century

Carp and Waterweeds

Start at the middle of the painting. The smaller carp points down toward the larger fish and a cluster of waterweeds. Follow the arc of each spine with your eye. Their bodies turn in different directions, so even the blank silk between them feels like a space they can swim through.

The writing above the pond belongs to another contributor, Mokumoku Dōjin. His poem recalls Qingao, a Daoist immortal associated with riding a carp. Text and image give the viewer two ways into the scene: the curve of a living fish and the memory of a story.

What gives the upper carp its direction?

Follow the narrowing head downward, then trace the body back up to the tail as it bends away. The outline carries the movement even where the brush barely describes the scales.

Image: The Metropolitan Museum of Art, public domain. Museum collection entry · 2015.300.60

Shibata Zeshin · Japan, 19th century

A Carp Ascending a Waterfall

Here the picture is taller than four of its widths. The water sweeps down in long bands while the fish faces up. Look for the eye beside one band of water and the tail farther down. Your gaze bridges the part of the body hidden by the cascade.

Near the top right, patterned fabric appears beside the falling water. Follow the boundaries between fabric, waterfall and fish. Zeshin plays with the vertical shape of the hanging scroll itself, drawing your eye up the same narrow route the carp appears to travel.

Where does the painting ask you to imagine the fish?

A band of water cuts across its body. The visible head and tail are enough for the viewer to join them, even though the full silhouette is never drawn.

Image: The Metropolitan Museum of Art, public domain. Museum collection entry · 2019.193.78

From a painted carp to a living koi

Try this beside a pond: choose one fish and follow its head, spine and tail through a turn. Then return to the scrolls. Each painter chose which details to show or leave to the imagination. These historical images tell us about ways of seeing carp; a modern variety name needs visible traits of the living fish.
